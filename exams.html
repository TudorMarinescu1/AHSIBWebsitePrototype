Assessments

Exams, assessment, and college credit

IB classes combine school-year internal assessments with externally evaluated work and May exams. The structure helps keep standards consistent across IB schools worldwide.

How Assessment Works

More than one May exam

IB assessment is spread across the course. Students complete classroom work, internal assessments, externally assessed tasks, and final exams. This means the grade is not based only on one test day; students build evidence of learning through several formats.

The assessment model is designed around set criteria. Students are not ranked against classmates. Instead, their work is evaluated against IB expectations for the subject, and work may be reviewed by examiners outside the school to support consistency across IB programs.

Key idea

Internal assessments happen during the year. External assessments and most written exams happen near the end of the course, typically in May.

Assessment Types

What students complete

Internal assessments

Each IB subject includes an internal assessment, often called an IA. Depending on the class, this may be a research paper, oral exam, presentation, lab investigation, historical investigation, performance element, film production work, or mathematical exploration. Teachers mark IAs using IB criteria, and samples can be submitted to IB for moderation.

External exams

Most IB courses end with written exams in May. These exams are proctored at school, then sent to IB for scoring by trained examiners. The questions usually ask students to apply knowledge, interpret sources or data, solve problems, and construct written responses rather than simply recall facts.

Externally assessed coursework

Some subjects include major work completed during the year and submitted externally instead of being scored only by the classroom teacher. Arts, language, and other performance or production-based courses may include supervised work, portfolios, recordings, or written components sent to IB examiners.

Core assessments

The Extended Essay and Theory of Knowledge are assessed separately from subject exams and can contribute bonus points. CAS is not scored with points, but it must be completed for diploma eligibility. Students should track core milestones alongside class assessments.

Scoring

How results are reported

IB subject scores use a 1-7 scale. The Extended Essay and Theory of Knowledge use letter grades that can add up to 3 bonus points. The full diploma is awarded with a minimum of 24 out of 45 possible points when all diploma conditions are met.

Registration and Timing

What happens during junior and senior year

Students register for IB assessments through the school. Juniors generally register for SL courses they are completing that year. Seniors register for all IB courses they are taking or finishing in senior year. Students should always confirm language-course timing and exceptions with the coordinator.

The busiest assessment windows often overlap with college applications, CAS reviews, Extended Essay drafts, TOK work, and final exam preparation. A strong plan spreads work across the two years and avoids treating May exams as the only deadline that matters.

Fall

Exam registration, senior college applications, IA topic selection, TOK planning, CAS progress checks, and EE drafting can overlap.

Winter

Students may complete oral work, research, lab drafts, TOK submissions, and Extended Essay milestones.

Spring

Internal assessment scores, predicted grades, submitted samples, CAS interviews, and May exams become the focus.

College Credit

How universities use IB scores

Colleges and universities set their own IB credit policies. UC campuses may award credit for a completed IB Diploma with qualifying scores and often award credit for individual Higher Level exams. Students should check policies for each college, major, and exam level because rules can differ by campus and department.
